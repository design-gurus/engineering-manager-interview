# The Project Retrospective Interview

A project retrospective interview is a round in which the candidate presents one past project and then defends its decisions under detailed questioning. The presentation takes 15 to 20 minutes, and the questions take the remaining 40. The round measures whether the candidate truly owned the project, understood it technically, and can describe its mistakes precisely.

Some companies call this round the project deep dive or the past project review. The format is the same.

## How the hour is spent

| Minutes | Activity | What the interviewer listens for |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 5 | Context: the business problem, the team, the timeline | Can the candidate explain why the project mattered? |
| 5 to 20 | The project: plan, key decisions, delivery | Which decisions belonged to the candidate? |
| 20 to 45 | Questions on decisions and alternatives | Depth, and honesty about what went wrong |
| 45 to 55 | Results and what the candidate would change | Measured outcomes and real lessons |
| 55 to 60 | Candidate's questions | Interest in how the company runs projects |

## Choosing the project

Score each candidate project from 0 to 2 on the five criteria below, and present the one with the highest total.

| Criterion | 0 points | 2 points |
|---|---|---|
| Recency | More than four years old | Within the last two years |
| Size | One or two engineers | Five or more engineers, or two or more teams |
| Your decisions | You inherited the plan | You made at least three decisions that changed the outcome |
| Difficulty | It went as planned | It had a real setback that you handled |
| Measured result | No numbers available | A before and after number you can state |

A project scoring 7 or more is a good choice. A project that went perfectly is a weak choice, because it gives the interviewer nothing to examine.

## A structure for the presentation

1. **Problem.** One sentence on the business need and one number that sized it.
2. **Team and constraints.** Headcount, deadline, and dependencies on other teams.
3. **Three decisions.** For each: the options, the one you chose, and the reason.
4. **The setback.** What went wrong, when you learned of it, and what you changed.
5. **Result.** The measured outcome against the original goal.
6. **Lessons.** Two things you now do differently.

**Example of one decision, stated well:** "We had 14 weeks and three options for the migration. A full rewrite was estimated at 22 weeks. Running both systems side by side fit in 12 but doubled the on-call load for a quarter. I chose the second option and negotiated one extra engineer for on-call."

## Questions to expect after the presentation

- "Why that option and not the other one?"
- "Who disagreed with you, and how was it settled?"
- "What did the timeline look like at the halfway point?"
- "What was the hardest technical problem, and who solved it?"
- "If you started again, what would you do differently?"
- "How did you keep the team informed when the plan changed?"

More questions are listed in [Project and delivery questions](../questions/project-and-delivery-questions.md).

## Common mistakes

- Spending 15 minutes on context and reaching the decisions with no time left.
- Being unable to explain the architecture one level below the diagram.
- Describing the setback as someone else's fault.
- Giving a lesson that is too general to act on, such as "communicate more".
