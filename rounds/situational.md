# The Situational Round

A situational question describes a hypothetical situation and asks what the candidate would do: "Suppose your best engineer tells you she is leaving. What do you do?" A behavioral question asks what happened in the past, and a situational question asks for a decision now. The interviewer is measuring judgment: how the candidate gathers facts, weighs the people involved, and reaches a decision in a few minutes.

## Why companies use it

- Every candidate gets the same question, so answers are easy to compare.
- It works for first-time manager candidates who have few past stories.
- The interviewer can change a detail and see whether the answer changes with it.

## A five-step answer

| Step | What you say | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Clarify | Ask one or two questions about facts that would change the answer | 20 seconds |
| 2. Name what matters | State the two or three concerns you are balancing | 20 seconds |
| 3. Act now | Say what you would do in the first 24 hours | 40 seconds |
| 4. Act later | Say what you would do over the next weeks to prevent a repeat | 40 seconds |
| 5. Link to experience | Give one sentence on a similar real case, if you have one | 20 seconds |

Step 1 matters most. A candidate who answers without asking anything is graded as someone who acts on assumptions.

## A worked example

**Question:** "You discover that a feature your team shipped last week has been reporting wrong numbers to customers, and nobody has noticed yet. What do you do?"

1. **Clarify:** "How many customers are affected, and are the numbers used for billing?" Suppose the answer is 40 customers and no billing effect.
2. **Name what matters:** "Three things: stopping the wrong numbers, telling customers before they find out, and keeping the engineer who wrote it from being blamed."
3. **Act now:** "Within the hour I confirm the scope with the engineer and the on-call person, and we disable the feature or correct it. The same day I tell my manager and the support lead, and we send the 40 customers a short notice with corrected figures."
4. **Act later:** "Within a week I run a review without blame, and we add a check that compares the feature's output with the source data."
5. **Link:** "I handled a similar reporting defect two years ago, and telling customers first shortened the support load to three tickets."

## Common situations

| Group | Example |
|---|---|
| Unnoticed mistakes | A defect only you know about |
| Unfamiliar assignments | You are given a team in a technical area you do not know |
| Customer pressure | A customer demands a date that is not possible |
| Angry customers | A customer escalates to your vice president |
| Coworker behavior | An engineer reports inappropriate behavior by a peer |
| Team events | A resignation, a reorganization, a sudden cut in headcount |

The full list is in [Situational questions](../questions/situational-questions.md).

## Common mistakes

- Giving a single action when the situation needs both an immediate and a later step.
- Forgetting a group that is affected, most often customers or the manager's own manager.
- Handling a conduct complaint alone. The expected answer involves the human resources partner at once.
- Changing the whole answer when the interviewer objects, instead of explaining which fact would change it.
