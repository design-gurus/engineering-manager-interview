# STAR for Engineering Managers

STAR is a structure for answering questions about past experience. The letters stand for Situation, Task, Action, and Result. An engineer can pass with plain STAR. A manager answer needs three additions inside it: the people decision, the trade-off, and the measured team outcome.

## The structure and the time for each part

| Part | What it contains | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | The team, the project, and the problem, in two sentences | 20 seconds |
| Task | What you were responsible for | 10 seconds |
| Action | What you did, in order, with reasons | 90 seconds |
| Result | The measured outcome | 30 seconds |
| Lesson | One thing you now do differently | 15 seconds |

The whole answer takes under three minutes. Most weak answers spend 90 seconds on the Situation and leave 20 for the Action.

## The three manager additions

| Addition | Question it answers | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| The people decision | What did you decide about a specific person or about roles on the team? | Action |
| The trade-off | What did you give up, and who agreed to it? | Action |
| The measured team outcome | What changed for the team, in a number? | Result |

An answer with all three sounds like a manager speaking. An answer with none sounds like a senior engineer describing a project.

## A weak answer and a strong answer

**Question:** "Tell me about a time your team was missing its commitments."

**Weak answer:**

> "My team was struggling with delivery for a while. There were a lot of interruptions and the planning was not great. I worked with everyone to improve our process, we communicated better, and I helped out with some of the harder tickets myself. After a few months things were much better and the team was happier."

It names no person, no decision, and no number. The only concrete action is the manager doing engineering work.

**Strong answer:**

> "My team of seven had completed about 60 percent of its planned work for three sprints in a row. I looked at where the time went and found that two senior engineers were spending roughly 15 hours a week each on support requests from other teams. I made two decisions. First, I set up a weekly support rotation so that one person handled requests and six were protected. Second, I moved one senior engineer off a feature she wanted, so that she could lead the rotation's setup for a month. She was not happy about it, and I told her plainly that it would delay her project by four weeks and that I would make sure her work was credited in her review. The cost was that the feature shipped one sprint late, which I agreed with the product lead in advance. Over the next four sprints we completed between 85 and 90 percent of planned work, and support response time went from two days to four hours. I now measure interruption hours whenever completion drops, before I change any process."

| Addition | Where it appears in the strong answer |
|---|---|
| People decision | Moving one senior engineer to lead the rotation, and the conversation with her |
| Trade-off | A feature delayed by one sprint, agreed with the product lead |
| Measured team outcome | Completion from 60 to 85 or 90 percent; response time from two days to four hours |

## Checklist before the interview

- Each story has one number in the Situation and one in the Result.
- "I" is used for your decisions, and "we" for the team's work.
- The trade-off names who agreed to it.
- The answer takes between two and three minutes when spoken aloud.
- You can answer "What would you do differently?" for each story.
